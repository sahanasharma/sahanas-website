01 / explain the business problem

A good quote is more
than a selling price.

My central question is how a healthcare distribution startup can make more defensible pricing, quotation and procurement decisions when supplier costs, freight, demand and delivery timing are uncertain.

The proposed users are the people preparing quotations, comparing procurement alternatives and reviewing commercial opportunities. The system should make the cost basis, expected contribution, cash requirement and uncertainty visible beside each decision.

Scope boundary: this is a commercial decision-support project. It does not make clinical recommendations or use patient records.

02 / describe the existing workflow

Map the work before
claiming to improve it.

The first task is to document how supplier information, quotations, enquiries and purchasing decisions currently move through Solivia. I will use stakeholder input and available records to identify genuine difficulties.

Supplier information→Quotation→Enquiry / order→Procurement & delivery

No baseline workflow study is attached to the plan. I therefore do not assume a particular spreadsheet setup, invent delays or assign an efficiency gain before measuring the existing process.

03 / document the data design

Give every number
a source.

The plan calls for linked SQL records covering products, suppliers, prices, minimum order quantities, lead times, quotations and enquiries. A proposed relational structure could group the information as follows; this is a design sketch, not a deployed database.

Products & supplier offersProduct IDs, supplier IDs, units, quantity tiers, currencies and validity dates.
Distributors & opportunitiesEnquiry IDs, requested quantities, stages, next actions and estimated values.
Quotations & quote linesLinked products, quantity, delivery basis, cost components and assumptions.
Orders & deliveriesConfirmed quantities, dated events, lead times and fulfilment records.

Stable identifiers should link these records. Transaction lines should be separated from document headers, and units, currencies, source references and dates retained rather than inferred later.

Observed

Supported by an actual quotation, order, record or documented event.

Estimated

A stated assumption or range, such as an unverified conversion probability.

Synthetic

A constructed example used to demonstrate or stress-test a method.

What the data audit will quantify

Coverage

Number of unique products, suppliers and completed orders; first and last usable dates; missing periods by product.

Completeness

Required populated fields ÷ required field slots × 100%. A missing sale is not automatically a zero sale.

Integrity

Duplicate primary keys, unmatched references, inconsistent units and records lacking a source or date.

Lead-time statistics

Count, mean, median and spread of observed lead times, with the event definition and units stated.

These are proposed audit measures. The plan supplies no production row counts or observed summary statistics, so none are invented here.

04 / explain the decision methodology

Make the reasoning
inspectable.

The following equations turn the plan into a proposed quantitative specification. They are methodological additions, not evidence that the full model has been built. The browser lab implements the simplified quotation equations only.

A. Landed cost, quotation and contribution

For an order of q units, let c be the supplier cost per unit, x the exchange rate into SGD, F total freight and I total insurance. In the simplified demonstration:

Total included cost, in SGD
C(q) = q × c × x + F + I
Landed cost per unit
cL = C(q) / q
Required price for target margin m
P* = cL / (1 − m)
Contribution and contribution margin
CM = qP − C(q)
Margin = CM / (qP)

Why divide by (1 − m)? Because margin is measured against revenue: m = (P − cL) / P. Rearranging gives P(1 − m) = cL. Multiplying cost by 1 + m would instead apply a markup.

The lab rounds the required price up to the nearest cent to preserve the selected minimum margin. It uses that quoted price for revenue and contribution, with full-precision costs underneath.

Model boundary: quantity is positive and the target margin is below 100%. All units are assumed sold. Only separately payable freight and insurance should be entered, so delivery charges already included in a supplier price are not counted twice. Other variable costs, taxes, overhead and payment timing are not modelled in the public example. A live quotation would need those inputs checked.

B. MR: does selling more add enough value?

Marginal revenue (MR) is the change in total revenue per additional unit sold. For two discrete commercial scenarios, compare changes in total revenue and total relevant cost, not just the new price. [2]

MR = (R₂ − R₁) / (q₂ − q₁)
MC = (C₂ − C₁) / (q₂ − q₁)
ΔCM = (R₂ − C₂) − (R₁ − C₁)
Synthetic scenario A

1,000 units at S$20

Total revenue: S$20,000
Total relevant cost: S$12,500
Contribution: S$7,500

Synthetic scenario B

1,200 units at S$19

Total revenue: S$22,800
Total relevant cost: S$14,400
Contribution: S$8,400

In this constructed comparison, MR = S$2,800 / 200 = S$14 per additional unit, MC = S$1,900 / 200 = S$9.50, and contribution rises by S$900. MR is below the new S$19 price because the lower price applies to every unit in scenario B.

Quantities, prices and costs are assumptions, not Solivia observations. This is a finite scenario comparison, not an estimated demand curve or proof that extra demand exists. A continuous model could use R(q) = qP(q) and MR = dR/dq, but that would require a justified demand specification.

C. Statistics: evaluate later observations

The source plan gates forecasting on data readiness. Where evidence is sufficient, compare simple baselines with a justified alternative using later observations not used to fit the model. For error et = yt − ŷt: [3]

MAE = Σ|et| / n
RMSE = √(Σet² / n)
Bias = Σet / n

MAE expresses average absolute error in the original units. RMSE gives large misses more influence. Positive bias under this sign convention means underprediction. Model choices should be made on training/validation data, with a final test reserved for evaluation.

D. Inventory and commercial risk

A proposed inventory rule links the reorder point to demand over the replenishment lead time. In a simplified deterministic illustration:

ROP = Expected lead-time demand + Safety stock

Assume 40 units per day, a 7-day lead time and an explicitly assumed 60-unit buffer. The illustrative reorder point is 40 × 7 + 60 = 340 units. The buffer is a scenario input, not an estimated service-level guarantee.

In the full project, safety stock should be justified by observed demand and lead-time uncertainty where possible. When the observations are insufficient, use labelled ranges rather than presenting chosen buffers as statistically estimated.

Sales concentration

Distributor share = that distributor’s confirmed sales ÷ total confirmed sales. Report pipeline shares separately; a zero denominator means the sales share is undefined.

Concentration index

HHI = Σsi², using shares as fractions between 0 and 1. This proposed indicator summarises concentration; no risk thresholds are asserted.

Weighted pipeline

Σ(probability × opportunity value). Every conversion probability is an assumption unless supported by evidence. This is not booked revenue.

Peak cash exposure

Maximum cumulative cash shortfall over the stated timeline. It requires payment dates and terms, not just a contribution-margin calculation.

E. Optimisation: choose a feasible order

The plan proposes constrained procurement optimisation. One possible single-period deterministic model is shown below, with zero starting inventory and fixed scenario prices and demand. It is a proposed specification, not an implemented solver.

For product i, let qi be ordered units, si units sold and yi a binary order indicator. Let p be selling price, c unit purchase cost, h additional holding cost per unsold unit, and k a fixed order cost.

Maximise scenario contribution after relevant inventory costs
max Σ[pisi − ciqi − hi(qi − si) − kiyi]
Subject to supported constraints
0 ≤ si ≤ di,   si ≤ qi
MOQiyi ≤ qi ≤ Uiyi
Σ(ciqi + kiyi) ≤ B
Σviqi ≤ V
qi, si are non-negative integers; yi ∈ {0, 1}

Here d is scenario demand, U a valid order upper bound, B the cash budget, v storage per unit and V total storage capacity. Purchase costs already cover all ordered units; h adds holding cost rather than charging for unsold units a second time.

Under optional purchasing, the zero-order solution is feasible. A “no feasible solution” test only makes sense once supported mandatory commitments or other incompatible constraints are introduced. The application should explain such conflicts rather than output an impossible order.

The full evaluation would compare the optimiser with simpler ordering rules under the same scenarios, examining contribution, stockouts, excess inventory and cash exposure. A stochastic extension or Monte Carlo stress test would require explicit probability assumptions.

All included costs are assumed payable upfront; no customer deposit is assumed. Cash headroom is therefore budget minus included cost. This is not a full working-capital timeline. Other costs are excluded, not silently estimated.

Read the default scenario from start to finish

Inputs: 1,000 units, supplier cost 12 per unit, exchange rate 1.00, freight S$400, insurance S$100 and target margin 25%.

Calculation: C = 1,000 × 12 × 1 + 400 + 100 = S$12,500. Cost per unit is S$12.50. Required unrounded price is 12.50 / 0.75 = S$16.666…; the rounded-up quotation is S$16.67.

Output: revenue S$16,670, contribution S$4,170 and actual quoted margin 25.015% before display rounding. With a S$15,000 budget, the included upfront cost leaves S$2,500 of headroom.

Sensitivity: increase freight by S$200 while leaving all other inputs unchanged. Total included cost becomes S$12,700 and the new minimum price is S$16.94. At the original S$16.67 price, contribution would instead fall by S$200 and margin to about 23.82%. An input assumption changes the required quote.

All business inputs in this walkthrough are constructed for demonstration. The dynamic lab may show different values after editing; this walkthrough always describes the stated defaults.

07 / evaluate results and business value

Measure what changed.
Do not guess the benefit.

No measured commercial outcomes are provided in the source plan. The completed case study should report what testing actually establishes, with the denominator, test set and assumptions visible.

01

Calculation checks

Pass rate = passed cases / tested cases. Compare the engine with hand calculations, boundary cases, missing inputs and supplier-tier transitions. State the rounding rule and tolerance.

02

Forecast evaluation

Report MAE, RMSE and bias on later observations. Include sample size, forecast horizon and baseline performance. Defer claims where the series cannot support evaluation.

03

Decision quality

Compare contribution, stockouts, excess inventory and cash exposure under identical scenarios. Keep simulated improvements distinct from observed outcomes.

04

Workflow evidence

Record stakeholder feedback and, where tested, comparable task times. Time change = (baseline − system time) / baseline × 100%, with sample size and task conditions stated.

Current evidence status: the plan defines intended work. Website demonstrations illustrate calculations. Neither establishes increased revenue, realised cost savings, a production forecast or validated operational improvements.

08 / discuss limitations and finish the report

The limits belong
beside the result.

Thin sales history, missing dates, inconsistent product definitions and uncertain lead times may limit forecasting. An optimiser can only choose within its stated objective and constraints; a numerically attractive order is not useful when important business restrictions are absent.

The public examples omit taxes, other variable costs, credit terms, overhead and interactions between products. A synthetic dataset can demonstrate logic, but cannot validate Solivia’s actual demand or commercial performance.

The final report should state what is operational versus experimental, document assumptions and sources, show selected anonymised workflows, and link to reproducible code only when the relevant repository is ready. Until then, this site links to my GitHub profile, not an invented project repository.

Phase 1 · 6 steps · 36 to 56 hours

Working commercial system

Organise the evidence, make the calculations transparent, then connect the workflow.

1.1Create the product and supplier database+

Inventory the information Solivia actually possesses, then organise products, suppliers, prices, minimum order quantities, lead times, quotations and enquiries into linked SQL tables. Record units, currencies, sources and dates. Separate observed information from estimates, and create a sanitised dataset for the public demonstration.

Hard rigour8 to 12 hours
1.2Build the landed-cost and margin calculator+

Calculate per-unit procurement, freight, insurance and other applicable variable costs without double-counting them. Calculate contribution margin and cash required. Document the assumptions behind each calculation, and manually check several examples against the system’s output.

Medium rigour5 to 8 hours
1.3Build the quotation scenario engine+

Allow a user to select a product, quantity, destination, delivery basis and target margin. Return a calculated quotation, cost breakdown and expected contribution. Compare supplier pricing tiers and order quantities, flag missing inputs and show when a quotation fails the selected margin requirement.

Hard rigour5 to 8 hours
1.4Create the distributor and tender-opportunity tracker+

Track each enquiry through quotation, documentation, purchase order, production and delivery stages. Include requested quantities, next actions, estimated deal value and expected timing. Clearly distinguish confirmed orders from opportunities; label conversion probabilities as assumptions rather than established facts.

Medium rigour5 to 8 hours
1.5Build the commercial-risk dashboard+

Show supplier, distributor and product concentration, tender dependence, working-capital exposure and outstanding documentation. Define each indicator and explain what it measures. Keep actual sales concentration separate from projected pipeline concentration so the dashboard does not imply revenue that has not occurred.

Medium rigour5 to 8 hours
1.6Connect everything in an interactive scenario laboratory+

Let users adjust quantities, supplier prices, freight, exchange rates and margins, then see the effects on quotations, contribution and cash requirements. Test the connected workflow with a Solivia stakeholder, check invalid inputs and correct usability problems. Keep confidential information out of the public demonstration.

Hard rigour8 to 12 hours

Phase 2 · 6 steps · 50 to 80 hours

Forecasting and procurement optimisation

Only forecast when the data supports evaluation. Compare decisions, not just models.

2.1Decide whether the data is ready for forecasting+

Review completed orders, available history, missing periods, product consistency and lead-time records. Decide which products can support genuine forecast evaluation. Where the evidence is insufficient, defer forecasting or use an explicitly labelled simulation, not a claimed prediction of Solivia’s sales.

Medium rigour4 to 6 hours
2.2Build and compare demand forecasts+

Start with simple baselines, such as previous-period demand or a moving average. Add a more complex method only where justified. Evaluate predictions using later observations that were not used to fit the model, examine forecast errors and bias, and explain the model-selection decision.

Hard rigour10 to 16 hours
2.3Translate uncertainty into inventory rules+

Estimate plausible demand and lead-time uncertainty. Use this to compare reorder points and safety-stock policies. Explain the trade-off between availability, excess stock and tied-up cash. Distinguish uncertainty estimated from observations from ranges chosen purely for scenario testing.

Hard rigour8 to 12 hours
2.4Build the constrained procurement optimiser+

Define order quantities as decision variables and choose a clearly stated objective, such as expected contribution after relevant inventory costs. Add supported constraints: budget, minimum order quantities, storage or supplier restrictions. Test whether the recommendations are feasible and whether the model handles cases with no feasible solution.

Hard rigour12 to 20 hours
2.5Stress-test the procurement policies+

Compare the optimiser against simpler ordering rules under the same demand and supplier scenarios. Use scenario analysis and, where justified, Monte Carlo simulation to vary demand, delays, freight and tender outcomes. Compare contribution, stockouts, excess inventory and cash exposure, not just forecast accuracy.

Hard rigour10 to 16 hours
2.6Integrate and validate the recommendations+

Add forecasts, inventory rules and procurement comparisons to the application. Show assumptions and uncertainty beside each recommendation. Review results with a Solivia stakeholder, investigate unrealistic outputs and record changes. Distinguish simulated improvements from any benefits observed during actual use.

Hard rigour6 to 10 hours
